AB 956: Can You Build Two Detached ADUs on Your Property Starting in 2027?

By Alex Fong · November 17, 2026

Since 2020, California has required cities and counties to approve one detached, new-construction ADU on most single-family lots without discretionary review. Starting January 1, 2027, that number goes to two. Assembly Bill 956 was signed on September 29, 2026, and is now Chapter 791, Statutes of 2026.

For homeowners with a larger lot, that can mean two rental units, or space for family members and a rental. But the law comes with conditions, and it changes how your options fit together. Here's what changed and what to check before you start design.

What AB 956 changes

  • Two detached ADUs, approved ministerially. On a lot with an existing or proposed single-family home, the city or county must approve up to two detached, new-construction ADUs that meet state standards. "Ministerial" means approval is based on objective rules, with no public hearing or case-by-case discretion.
  • Four-foot side and rear setbacks. Each detached ADU can sit as close as four feet from the side and rear property lines.
  • Local size and height limits still apply. Your city or county can cap each of these units at 800 square feet of livable space and apply the state's ADU height limits.
  • No short-term rentals. If these ADUs are rented, the rental terms must be longer than 30 days.
  • Stronger protection from HOA restrictions. See the HOA section below.

The trade-off: two detached ADUs or a junior ADU

Many summaries of AB 956 leave this out. If you build two detached ADUs under this rule, the city is not required to approve a junior ADU (JADU) on the same lot. A JADU is a unit of up to 500 square feet created inside the main house.

So if you think you might eventually want a JADU, for example converting part of the house for a family member, plan the whole property at once. Choosing two detached units now can limit what you're able to add later.

If you live in an HOA

State law already voided HOA rules that prohibit or unreasonably restrict ADUs. AB 956 extends that protection to any lot zoned to allow single-family use, not only lots zoned for single-family use. That closes a gap that some associations relied on. HOAs can still apply reasonable restrictions, such as design standards. However, those restrictions can't unreasonably increase construction costs or effectively prevent construction, and the HOA can't charge fees to approve an ADU.

Allowed doesn't mean it fits

The law sets what a city must approve. It doesn't make every lot suitable for two units. These are the issues that usually decide whether a second detached ADU is practical:

  • Usable space. After setbacks, easements, existing structures, trees and slopes, is there room for two units, plus walkways and outdoor space each tenant would reasonably expect?
  • Access. Construction equipment, fire department access, and a reasonable path from the street to each unit all take space. Rear-lot units are often limited by access more than by square footage.
  • Water service and fire sprinklers. Two additional units add demand to the water meter. Depending on the distance to a fire hydrant and local fire requirements, fire sprinklers may be required, and they can require a larger meter or a new service. This is one of the most commonly overlooked costs.
  • Sewer. Whether both units can connect to the existing sewer lateral depends on elevations, distance and the local district's requirements. Properties on septic have a separate set of limits.
  • Electrical service. Two all-electric units can exceed the capacity of the existing panel or service. A service upgrade, or separate meters, adds cost and coordination with the utility.
  • Drainage and grading. More roof and paved area means more runoff to manage, especially on sloped lots.

A second unit won't double the cost of the first. Some site work, utility upgrades and design can be shared. But utilities in particular can add costs that scale quickly, so it pays to understand them before committing to a layout.

What this means in the Greater Sacramento area

State ADU law sets the minimum that every jurisdiction must allow. Cities and counties such as Sacramento, Roseville, Rocklin and Placer County may take time to update their local ADU ordinances. Starting January 1, 2027, the state rules apply whether or not the local ordinance has been updated.

If you're planning two detached units, ask your planning department which rules will apply to your application, based on when it's submitted. Also ask how they're handling AB 956 before their ordinance is updated. Water, sewer and fire requirements are set by local districts and vary by property, so check those early too.

Where to start

Before paying for design, confirm three things: the buildable area after setbacks and easements, whether your water, sewer and electrical service can support two more units, and what your jurisdiction will require. Those answers determine whether two units make sense, and roughly what they'll cost.
