How Much Does an ADU Cost in the Sacramento Area?

Typical ranges by ADU type, what moves the number, and the costs that are hardest to know early.

By Alex Fong · November 20, 2026

The short answer

There's no single price for an ADU. Published 2026 estimates for the Sacramento area range from about $50,000 for a small junior ADU to $300,000 or more for a larger detached unit. Where a project falls depends on three things: the type of ADU, its size, and the conditions on your lot.

Cost-per-square-foot numbers are a starting point, not a budget. Two ADUs with the same floor plan can differ by tens of thousands of dollars because of fees, utility work and site conditions. This article explains what drives the cost and which costs are hardest to pin down early.

Typical ranges by type of ADU

The type of ADU sets the baseline. Converting space that already has a roof, walls and a foundation usually costs far less than building a new structure from the ground up.

ADU typePublished rangeWhat drives the cost
Junior ADU (JADU), up to 500 sq ft$50,000–$100,000Built inside the existing home; often shares utilities
Garage conversion$80,000–$150,000Existing structure; new insulation, windows, plumbing, possibly slab work
Attached ADU$100,000–$300,000New foundation and framing tied into the main house
Detached ADU$150,000–$300,000+New foundation, full utility runs, site work and solar

Ranges are third-party estimates published in 2026 by Block Renovation. They are general planning figures, not Calterra Homes pricing or a quote for any project.

Why smaller isn't always cheaper per foot. A kitchen and a bathroom cost about the same whether the unit is 450 or 900 square feet. In a small unit those fixed costs are spread over fewer square feet, so the cost per square foot goes up.

Where the money goes

Construction is the largest share of an ADU budget, but it isn't the whole budget. A complete budget usually includes:

  • Design and engineering: plans, structural engineering and the Title 24 energy report.
  • Permits and fees: building permit and plan review, plus impact and school fees depending on size.
  • Site work: clearing, grading, drainage and access.
  • Utilities: water, sewer and electrical connections, and any upgrades they require.
  • Construction: foundation, framing, roofing, mechanical, electrical, plumbing and finishes.
  • Solar: generally required on new detached ADUs under California's energy code.
  • Contingency: a reserve for conditions that show up once work starts.

From the field: Grading and utilities are the two costs I see underestimated most often. Grading depends on the topography where the ADU will sit. It's hard to price accurately until someone looks closely at the site, so early budgets tend to come in low.

Permit and impact fees: the 750-square-foot line

In California, size affects whether your ADU pays impact fees. State law bars cities, special districts and water companies from charging impact fees on an ADU with 750 square feet of interior living space or less (Gov. Code § 66311.5). Above that size, impact fees can apply, and in some cities they add thousands of dollars.

A change coming in 2027. A new state law, SB 1117, is set to limit impact fees on ADUs over 750 square feet to the square footage above 750, starting January 1, 2027 (Holland & Knight summary). How each city applies it is worth confirming. For more on recent ADU law, see our article on AB 956 and two detached ADUs.

School fees are separate. School districts set and collect their own fees. Under state law, ADUs of 500 square feet or less are generally exempt from school impact fees (Lozano Smith). Larger units pay the district's rate.

Every agency is different. Fees differ between the City of Sacramento, Sacramento County, Placer County, El Dorado County and each city within them. Water and sewer are often handled by separate districts with their own charges. Get a current fee estimate from every agency that serves your address before you finalize a budget.

Utility costs that surprise people

Utilities are where ADU budgets most often go off track. The problems are underground or inside the electrical panel, and they usually don't show up until someone checks.

  • Electrical panel and service. An ADU adds a kitchen, a bathroom, heating and cooling, and sometimes an EV charger. Many older homes need a panel upgrade or new service from SMUD or PG&E to carry that load.
  • Water. Some ADUs can share the main home's water line. Others need a larger line or meter, depending on the water district and the demand.
  • Sewer. The ADU usually ties into the main home's sewer line. If the ADU sits lower than the line, or the line is old or damaged, a pump or new lateral may be needed.
  • Distance. Every foot of trench between the main house and the ADU adds cost. A unit at the back of a deep lot costs more to connect than one near the house.
  • Fire protection. Whether fire sprinklers are required depends on the main home, local fire rules and the site. We'll cover this in a separate article.

From the field: Utility costs usually come down to two questions. Where can we connect, and is enough capacity available there? Often neither answer is known at the start of a project. That's why utilities are one of the biggest variables in an ADU budget, and why we check them before design begins.

How to keep ADU costs under control

The biggest savings come from decisions made before design starts.

  1. Decide on size with the fee lines in mind. At 750 square feet or less, impact fees generally don't apply. At 500 or less, school fees generally don't apply either.
  2. Put the kitchen and bathroom near existing utilities. Shorter water, sewer and electrical runs cost less.
  3. Keep the shape simple. A rectangle with a simple roof costs less to engineer, frame and roof than a design with many corners.
  4. Look at what you already have. A garage or underused space in the house may convert for much less than new construction.
  5. Ask about pre-approved plans. Some cities offer ADU plans that have already been reviewed, which can shorten design and plan check.
  6. Carry a contingency. Set money aside for conditions found once work starts.

Check the property before you pay for plans

Most cost surprises can be found before design begins. A feasibility study looks at zoning, setbacks, utility availability and capacity, access, fees and site conditions. It gives you a realistic picture of the project before you spend money on drawings. Our guide on how to start planning an ADU covers the first steps.

A feasibility study is the first phase of every Calterra Homes ADU project. If the numbers don't work, you find out early, while it still costs little to change course.

Common questions

Is a garage conversion always the cheapest option?
Usually, but not always. An old slab, low ceilings or a garage far from the sewer line can push the cost toward a small new build.

Does a prefab ADU save money?
It can shorten the schedule. You still pay for the foundation, utility connections, permits, delivery and setting the unit, which together can be a large share of the total.

Will I need a separate electric meter?
Not necessarily. Many ADUs share the main home's service. A separate meter is possible, but it usually costs more and depends on the utility. We'll cover this in a later article.

How long does it take?
Design, permitting and construction together commonly take many months to a year or more, depending on the type of ADU, the jurisdiction and the site.
