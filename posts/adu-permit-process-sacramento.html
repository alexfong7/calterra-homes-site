The ADU Permit Process in Greater Sacramento, Step by Step

What happens between "we want an ADU" and "you can move in," and where the time usually goes.

By Alex Fong · November 23, 2026

The short version

In California, an ADU that meets state and local standards gets a ministerial permit. That means the city or county checks your plans against the rules, without a public hearing or a vote. The process is the same basic sequence everywhere in the Sacramento region: confirm what your lot allows, prepare plans, submit, respond to corrections, get the permit, build, and pass inspections.

The details differ by city and county. That includes who reviews your plans, which forms you use, and which water, sewer and fire agencies get involved. The steps below cover what is common, with notes on where local rules come in.

1Confirm what your lot allows

Before anyone draws plans, check the basics for your address:

  • Zoning and ADU rules: size limits, height, setbacks and how many ADUs are allowed.
  • Easements and recorded restrictions: utility easements can block where a unit goes.
  • Utilities: where water, sewer and power can connect, and whether there is enough capacity.
  • Access and site conditions: slope, trees, drainage and how equipment gets to the back of the lot.
  • HOA rules, if you have an HOA. They can set reasonable design standards but generally can't prohibit an ADU.

This is the work of a feasibility study. It's the cheapest point in the project to find a problem.

2Choose custom plans or preapproved plans

You can hire a designer to draw custom plans, or start from a set of plans the city or county has already reviewed.

State law (AB 1332) requires every city and county to run a program for preapproving ADU plans and to post those plans online. Two local examples:

  • City of Sacramento: offers free permit-ready plans for detached ADUs, plus a list of other preapproved plans (City of Sacramento).
  • Sacramento County: offers free "Shelf Ready" detached ADU plans for properties in unincorporated areas of the county (Sacramento County).

Preapproved plans can save design time and money, and state law gives agencies a shorter review window for them. The trade-off is flexibility. Programs generally limit changes to the floor plan, and you still need a site plan showing where the unit goes on your lot. Utility connections, grading and site work are still specific to your property.

3Put together the permit set

For a typical new ADU, the submittal includes:

  • A site plan showing the ADU, the main house, setbacks, easements and utility routes
  • Architectural drawings: floor plans, elevations, sections and details
  • Structural drawings and calculations
  • A Title 24 energy compliance report
  • Electrical, plumbing and mechanical information
  • Solar plans, when the energy report requires solar
  • The agency's application forms

Every agency has its own checklist. Use it. A missing item is the most common reason a submittal is sent back before review even starts.

4Submit and get a completeness check

Most agencies in the region take ADU applications electronically. Under state law, the agency has 15 business days to tell you in writing whether your application is complete. If it isn't, the notice should list what's missing.

5Plan review and corrections

Once the application is complete, reviewers check the plans. Depending on the agency, that can include building, planning, fire, public works and utilities staff.

State law sets a deadline. The agency must approve or deny a complete ADU application within 60 days, or it is deemed approved (Gov. Code § 66317). For a detached ADU using a preapproved plan, the window is generally 30 days.

In practice, most projects get at least one round of correction comments. Your designer revises the plans and resubmits. Clear, complete drawings are the best way to keep correction rounds short.

6Clear the other agencies

The building department is not the only agency involved. Depending on your address, you may also need sign-offs or payments from:

  • The water district and the sewer district, which may be separate from the city
  • The fire department or fire district
  • The school district, for school fees on larger units
  • SMUD or PG&E, if the electrical service needs an upgrade or a new meter

Start these early. Utility coordination often runs on its own timeline, separate from plan review.

7Pay fees and pick up the permit

Once plans are approved and other agency requirements are met, you pay the remaining fees and the permit is issued. Fees depend on the agency, the ADU's size and your location. Our article on what an ADU costs in the Sacramento area explains how size affects impact and school fees.

8Build and pass inspections

Construction is inspected in stages. Each stage has to pass before the work is covered up. A typical sequence:

StageWhat the inspector checks
FoundationForms, rebar and anchor bolts before concrete is poured
Underground utilitiesSewer, water and electrical lines before trenches are backfilled
Rough framing and systemsFraming, plus rough plumbing, electrical and mechanical
InsulationInsulation and energy details before drywall
FinalFinished work, safety items and any solar system

Inspection names and order vary by agency. Your permit card lists the required inspections.

A passed final inspection is when the ADU is officially complete and can be occupied.

How long does it take?

There's no single answer. The state deadlines cover the agency's review time, not your design time, correction rounds, utility coordination or construction. A garage conversion with simple utilities can move quickly. A detached ADU on a sloped lot with a long utility run takes longer.

The projects that move fastest usually have three things in common: the lot was checked before design, the plans were complete when submitted, and utility questions were answered early.

Common questions

Do I need a public hearing for an ADU?
Generally no. ADUs that meet the rules are approved ministerially, without a hearing.

Can I pull the permit myself?
Homeowners can often apply as owner-builders. That makes you responsible for the work, the inspections and any workers on the job. Most homeowners have a licensed contractor or designer handle the submittal.

What if I live in a city, not the county?
Each city runs its own permit process. Roseville, Rocklin, Folsom, Elk Grove and the other cities in the region each have their own building department, forms and preapproved plans. Unincorporated areas are handled by the county.

Do the new 2027 ADU laws change the process?
Several ADU laws take effect January 1, 2027. We'll cover them in an upcoming article. For one recent change, see our article on AB 956 and two detached ADUs.
